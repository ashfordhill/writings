# Roadmap to Vienna

Standing at 5'2", I’ve always had trouble finding clothes that fit. I used to shop in the boys’ section, which was nothing short (har har) of emasculating. Clothing stores made me anxious and ashamed.

Of course I had heard of tailors, but that seemed a little weird. No one else I knew needed everything tailored. Wasn’t that just for formal wear? Why did I have to pay more simply because I had a small body?

Eventually, I got over myself and visited Abel’s tailor shop.

Abel is a wonderful tailor with a fantastic eye, located just a mile from my house. He moved to the United States from Mexico by himself as a teenager and has worked hard ever since. This year, he and his wife (who also works at the shop) started taking Sundays off for the first time in 25 years. The man works hard! I respect that.

During a recent visit, I started complaining to him about the stupid inefficiencies I see at work.

“I’m tired of this. I’m tired of that. I’m tired of working for other people!”

Abel stopped me.

“Wait a second, Ash. There are always problems, no matter which side you’re on.”

Sometimes, he told me, he wished he had a regular 9-to-5. The work of owning a business never really ends, even when the shop is closed. Looking back, he wishes he had been able to devote more time and money to other parts of his life - including planning for retirement.

It wasn’t what I wanted to hear; that was probably why I needed to hear it.

## The source of advice matters

Older people can offer valuable advice because they’ve had time to live through the consequences of their decisions. Age alone doesn’t make someone wise, but it does give them more evidence to work with.

The source still matters.

What experiences shaped this person’s beliefs? What kind of company do they keep in their personal and professional life? What principles guide their decisions? Is their advice supported by the way they actually live?

Character is revealed through actions, not whatever words someone can spin out of their mouth. People can lead each other down destructive paths without intending to. **Good intentions aren’t enough.**

Abel’s advice carried weight because it wasn’t theoretical. He has spent decades living a version of independence I was romanticizing.

He wasn’t telling me *not* to build something of my own. He was warning me the grass isn't greener on the other side. If I believe this to be true, then I can be more patient about the road ahead.

## Playing the long game

I’m a skilled, high-potential worker. On a good day, my 30% can resemble someone else’s 110%. I’m learning that I don’t need to give 100% of myself to a company in which I have no stake. Hellllll nah.

If I decide to build a business, my roadmap looks something like this:

- Secure a recession-resilient job that leaves me with enough time and energy to have a life. Ideally, it would be remote or have a short commute. Long commutes drain my quality of life.

- Stop spending all my mental energy proving myself at that job. Dopamine rushes from wins are fleeting and will leave me with an endless, unquenched thirst.

- Surround myself with growth-minded, optimistic people I trust. Marinating in other people’s stagnation will affect me whether I want it to or not. Regularly chatting with Eeyore will eventually bring me down, and I don’t need that shit while trying to build something.

- Use the remaining energy to work consistently on side ventures. Once one becomes stable enough, I can consider moving toward it full-time without immediately piling business loans, health insurance, and every other financial pressure onto a brand-new idea.

Having an outlet for autonomy makes the 9-to-5 much more tolerable. The job feels less like my identity and more like: “I’m here for now, not forever. I’ve got bills to pay.” That approach may be slower, but **slower isn’t the same as stagnant.**

## I don’t want to build alone

There is still a significant gap in this plan: I want a partner.

I want someone I can lean on, support, and bounce ideas off. Humans can hardly accomplish anything entirely alone. No matter how capable or talented we are, we all have **blind spots**. Collaboration doesn’t eliminate that blindness, but it can reduce it.

I’ve been energized by supportive, additive people who have acted as kind of semi-partners. But I’m still waiting for a specific kind of partnership. One in which we can make something worthwhile, learn together, and meet mistakes with compassionate hope instead of judgmental blame.

That person may not live near me. Fortunately, distance feels more like a logistical problem than an actual barrier.

My job is remote, and there is allegedly a general fund that helps remote employees travel back to home base, or so I was told by a project engineer in Texas. I also have more than enough airline and hotel points to travel without touching my bank account, thanks to my husband’s credit card churning obsession.

When I first moved to Chicago, I still had orthodontic braces. I would fly to Colorado in the morning, see my orthodontist for an adjustment, and fly home that same day. Honestly, it wasn’t that bad.

If something, or someone, is worth showing up for I will find a way around logistical obstacles.
